A handful of sand.

It comes from deep in the ground at Madjedbebe, a rock shelter on Mirarr Country in Arnhem Land.

Scientists kept the sand in the dark. A buried grain of sand slowly stores up a tiny charge. Sunlight wipes the charge away. So the charge shows roughly how long ago the grain last saw the sun.

In 2017 the scientists published what the sand showed.

      Step 5

      Read, Ask, Decide

      How long have people lived in Australia, and how do we know?

      1. Read: what is agreed

      • All the sources in this app say First Nations people have lived in Australia for tens of thousands of years.
      • Scientists do not agree on exactly when people first arrived. One team says about 65,000 years ago. Another team says about 50,000 to 55,000 years ago.
      • The sea was much lower in the last Ice Age. Australia, New Guinea and Tasmania were one land.
      • The First Fleet arrived in 1788. That is years ago.

      2. Ask: three witnesses, three questions

      1. 1. What did they say they saw?
      2. 2. Could they really have seen it?
      3. 3. Why might they say it?

      The Madjedbebe team

      Scientists who dug at Madjedbebe · 2017

      “Human occupation began around 65,000 years ago …”

      In plain words: people were living at Madjedbebe around 65,000 years ago.

      Source. Chris Clarkson, Zenobia Jacobs, Ben Marwick and others, “Human occupation of northern Australia by 65,000 years ago”, Nature 547 (2017), 306–310.

      See the source (Nature) ↗

      The 2018 review team

      Scientists who checked the Madjedbebe reports · 2018

      “We find that an age estimate of >50 ka for this site is unlikely to be valid.”

      In plain words: they think “more than 50,000 years” for Madjedbebe is unlikely to be right. (“ka” means a thousand years.)

      Source. James F. O’Connell, Jim Allen, Martin A. J. Williams and others, “When did Homo sapiens first reach Southeast Asia and Sahul?”, PNAS 115, no. 34 (2018), 8482–8490.

      See the source (PNAS) ↗

      The Budj Bim World Heritage listing

      UNESCO World Heritage Committee · 2019

      “For the Gunditjmara, deep time refers to the idea that they have always been there. From an archaeological perspective, deep time refers to a period of at least 32,000 years that Aboriginal people have lived in the Budj Bim Cultural Landscape.”

      In plain words: the Gunditjmara say they have always been there. Archaeologists say people have lived there for at least 32,000 years.

      Source. UNESCO World Heritage Centre, “Budj Bim Cultural Landscape”, inscribed 2019, Statement of Outstanding Universal Value.

      ⏳ The sand clock

      A buried grain of sand slowly stores up a tiny charge. Sunlight wipes it away. Scientists measure the charge in the dark to work out how long ago the grain last saw the sun. This is called OSL, short for optically stimulated luminescence.

      This tells when the sand was buried. It does not tell who left the tools in it.

      Nature, 2017 ↗

      🐝 The wasp-nest clock

      Mud wasps build nests on rock walls. Scientists can date tiny bits of old nests. A nest under a painting is older than the painting. A nest on top is younger. That is how the Kimberley kangaroo painting was dated to about 17,300 years ago.

      Nature Human Behaviour, 2021 ↗University of Melbourne ↗

      🏝️ Alone on an island

      The National Museum of Australia quotes two historians about the people of Tasmania after the sea rose:

      “They were alone for five hundred generations, knowing no other people in the world, living with the knowledge and beliefs derived from those they had known before the sea surrounded them.”

      Source. Iain Davidson and David Roberts, “14 000 BP – On Being Alone: The Isolation of Tasmania”, in Turning Points in Australian History (2009), quoted by the National Museum of Australia.

      National Museum of Australia ↗

      🔤 Word bank
      • Sahul: scientists’ name for the one big land of Australia, New Guinea and Tasmania when the sea was low.
      • Megafauna: very large animals, such as Diprotodon.
      • Kooyang: the Gunditjmara word for the short-finned eel, used in the UNESCO listing.
      • Country: the land, waters and sky that a First Nations people belong to and care for.
      • Archaeologist: a scientist who studies the past from things people left behind.

      AIATSIS Code of Ethics ↗

      Cultural care. Mungo Lady and Mungo Man are named but not pictured; no images of human remains or deceased people are used. No rock art is reproduced or redrawn in this app. The Kimberley painting dated in 2021 is described only as a published scientific dating result, and is not shown. A public Wikimedia Commons photograph of Murujuga is linked below for teachers, not displayed on the student cards. The drawings are simple diagrams with no people.

      Points to flag with students.

      • Madjedbebe: about 65,000 years (Clarkson et al., Nature, 2017) against the 2018 review (O’Connell et al., PNAS), which puts arrival in the region at about 50,000–55,000 years. The debate continues in later papers.
      • Lake Mungo: Mungo Lady (cremated, then her bones buried) and Mungo Man (buried with red ochre) are both dated 40,000–42,000 years (National Museum of Australia). UNESCO’s Willandra Lakes listing gives evidence of occupation from 45,000–60,000 years ago. These describe different evidence.
      • Diprotodon: the Australian Museum gives extinction at about 25,000 years ago. Other studies give different dates, and the causes are debated.
      • Tasmania: the National Museum of Australia gives about 12,000 years; Williams et al. (2018) give about 11,000 years for the flooding of Bass Strait.
      • The Murujuga and Budj Bim cards give “at least” ages from UNESCO. The time walk places them at that age; the cultures continue to today.
      • The map is generated from NOAA ETOPO1 sea-floor depths sampled every 0.05 degrees. The first frame matches a sea level about 120 m lower. The Tasmania and New Guinea frames are matched to those events, not to exact past sea levels, because the coarse grid does not show the shallow straits accurately. Present-day depths also differ from past land heights.
      • The time comparisons use “at least 65,000 years” and the current year, so the numbers update each year. The toggle shows the 50,000-year version.
      • The witnesses are documents. The third witness reports a Gunditjmara view in UNESCO’s words, not in Gunditjmara people’s own words.
